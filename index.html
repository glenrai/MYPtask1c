Text A: "The Beach That Wasn't There"

An opinion column from the travel section of a weekend newspaper

1Last winter, exhausted after a year of deadlines, I saw the beach that would save me. It appeared on my phone at two in the morning: white sand, water the colour of a swimming pool, a single wooden hut under a palm tree. No crowds. No noise. The caption promised "the reset you deserve." I booked the flights before breakfast.

2The beach existed, technically. It was on the map, on the coast where the post said it would be. But the sand was grey, the water was the ordinary green-blue of the sea, and the wooden hut was a car park. A local café owner laughed when I showed her the picture. "Ah," she said. "Another one. We get three a week."

3The photo, it turned out, had been generated by artificial intelligence and shared by an account that earned money every time someone clicked its booking link. Nobody had lied to me in words. The picture had done the lying all by itself.

4I was angry, and then I was embarrassed, and then I was something worse: suspicious. Back home, I started doubting everything. A friend's sunset? Too orange. A hotel's swimming pool? Too blue. A smiling family in an advert? Too perfect, surely. Within a month I had stopped believing in anything beautiful.

5This is the hidden cost of fake images. We worry, rightly, about being fooled. We talk much less about what happens next: once we have been fooled, we stop trusting what is real. A genuinely stunning lake begins to look fake. A genuinely happy person begins to look like a computer's idea of happiness.

6So what should we do? Believing everything again is no answer. Asking better questions might be. Who made this image, and what do they want me to do? Is there a name, a place or a person I can check? Has anyone I trust actually been there?

7And perhaps we should ask ourselves a harder question: why was I so ready to believe? I was tired. I wanted an escape so badly that I let a picture make the decision for me. The advertisers knew that. They always do.

8In the end, I stayed the full week on the grey beach. I swam in the ordinary sea. I ate fried fish at the café, and the owner told me about the storm that had taken the old pier. It was nothing like the reset I had been promised. It was better, because it was real.

Worked example: use this as a model

Question: Interpret what the writer means by "The picture had done the lying all by itself" (paragraph 3).

Model answer: The writer means that no one wrote a false statement, yet the image still deceived the writer, because people trust what they see. The phrase "all by itself" suggests that pictures can mislead without anyone needing to say anything untrue. This makes the reader realise how easily images can persuade us.

Pattern: point (what the writer means or does), evidence (short quotation), effect (what it shows or how it affects the reader).

Model notes

The advertisement: Tourism New Zealand presents the country as a "wellness studio" for a week-long reset, with nature such as forests, coastlines and geothermal waters, plus adventure activities.

Who it targets and why: stressed, time-poor Americans; the US is one of New Zealand's biggest visitor markets.

The AI claims: some viewers online said the host looked too handsome and too polished to be a real person.

Tone of the report: light-hearted and playful, while still raising a serious question about trust in what we see.

Part C: compare and contrast essay

In the real eAssessment, Task 1E asks you to write a full compare-and-contrast essay. Today you write it paragraph by paragraph. For each paragraph, read what to include, choose the sentence starters that help you, and write the paragraph in your own words in the box.

Essay question: Compare and contrast how Text A ("The Beach That Wasn't There") and Text B (the news report on Tourism New Zealand's advertisement) present the difficulty of knowing what is real.

Before you write: the comparison grid (3 minutes)

Text A: the opinion columnText B: the news report
Model grid

Main message: A: fake images fool us and then make us stop trusting real things; we should ask better questions. B: AI is now so common that people doubt even a real person in an advert.

Purpose and audience: A: to persuade and warn newspaper readers. B: to inform a general TV audience about a news story, with some entertainment.

Evidence: A: "Within a month I had stopped believing in anything beautiful." B: viewers claiming the host is too perfect to be real; Webber's joke "Maybe I'm an alien".

Tone: A: personal, reflective, slightly bitter, then calm. B: light-hearted and playful, with a serious question underneath.

Effect on the audience: A: readers question their own habits and trust. B: viewers are amused but also made aware of how AI changes what we believe.

How a strong comparative paragraph works

Each body paragraph should discuss both texts. Use this pattern: point about both texts, evidence from Text A, evidence from Text B, comparison (similar or different?), effect on the audience.

Useful linking words: both, similarly, likewise, in the same way, however, in contrast, whereas, while, on the other hand, unlike, therefore, as a result, ultimately.
